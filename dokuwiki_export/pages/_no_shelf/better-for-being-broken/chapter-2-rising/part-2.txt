====== Part 2 ======

Driving became my obsession. It was more than just getting behind the wheel—it was the promise that I might one day feel normal again. It was freedom, control, and a tiny piece of my identity clawed back from the wreckage.
But learning to drive again wasn’t simple. My leg was weak and stiff, full of metal and scars. I had to practice moving from the gas to the brake without searing pain shooting up my spine. My husband took me out to empty parking lots, where I’d circle and circle, my palms sweating, determined not to let fear win. Each time I shifted my foot, I gritted my teeth and told myself: I am not done yet.
Meanwhile, the wounds on my leg were still healing. Bandages and ointments were part of my daily routine. Some days, the pain was so sharp that simply pulling the bedsheet over my leg felt like torture. The scars itched and pulled at my skin as if my body was trying to remind me of the violence it had endured.
Emotionally, I was swinging between rage and gratitude. Rage for the accident, for the surgeries, for losing pieces of my old life. Gratitude for the small things: a breeze through an open window, the feel of sunshine on my face, the taste of real food after months of hospital trays.
At night, I often lay awake replaying the crash over and over. The sound of metal hitting metal. The instant my leg shattered. The weight of the truck looming above me. But there were also nights when I allowed myself to imagine a future beyond this chapter. A future where I could walk into a room without crutches. Where I’d ride again—not necessarily a motorcycle, but something that made me feel the wind in my hair.
And slowly, piece by piece, I started believing that future was possible.
