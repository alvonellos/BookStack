====== Part 3 ======

The first time I drove all the way to the store by myself, I sat in the car afterward and cried. Not because of pain, but because for those few minutes, I felt like my old self again. Like I was a person with places to go, not just a patient surviving from appointment to appointment.
But even victories came with shadows. I couldn’t carry groceries into the house on my own. I couldn’t stand for long enough to cook dinner. There were days my leg swelled until my skin felt like it might split open. Every triumph was paired with a reminder that my body still held limits I couldn’t ignore.
The nightmares didn’t stop either. Some nights, I dreamed I was back under the truck, trapped, metal pressing into my bones. I’d wake up shaking, drenched in sweat, reaching down to feel my leg and reassure myself it was still there. Other nights, the sound of a passing truck outside the window would make my chest tighten until I couldn’t breathe.
But there was progress, too. I began going on short walks with my husband. At first, I used a walker. Then crutches. Then, tentatively, nothing at all. Each step felt like balancing on glass. My back ached, my pelvis throbbed, and my nerves screamed, but I kept going. Because moving forward, even in pain, felt better than standing still.
And as the weeks rolled by, I noticed something else: people began to see me again, not just the injuries. Strangers at stores smiled at me without a look of pity. Friends invited me places. My world started to expand beyond the walls of my house and the sterile corridors of hospitals and rehab centers.
I still carried grief for what I’d lost. I probably always will. But alongside it, a quiet hope was taking root. A belief that even if my body would never be exactly the same, my life could still be full, meaningful, and entirely my own.
