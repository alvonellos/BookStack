====== Part 1 ======

Leaving the hospital was supposed to feel like freedom, but it didn’t. It felt like being exiled into a world where nothing worked the way it used to. The hardest part, nobody warned me, was the wheelchair—the way it forced me to depend on someone else for everything, stripping me of privacy and control. I was trapped in the basement of our house because of the stairs, reduced to measuring my world in square feet instead of miles. Even the simplest things—getting in and out of the car for medical appointments—felt like scaling mountains.
Rehab wasn’t some cinematic triumph. There wasn’t one big turning point. It was a solid grind, day after relentless day, facing pain and fear and doubt. Every inch of progress was earned with sweat and tears.
My relationship with my husband changed in ways both beautiful and heartbreaking. The bad part was that he became my caretaker, and that blurred the lines of our marriage. He was gentle, patient, devoted—a good "Carrie," as I sometimes joked. But I also know now, without a shred of doubt, how far my husband will go for me. He stood in the trenches beside me when everything was at its worst, and that’s a kind of love few people ever get to see.
Some friendships surprised me. My family completely abandoned me once I came home from the hospital. It’s a wound that still aches. One friend visited me every week, refusing to let me fade away—but that friendship collapsed after his husband raped me a few months later. Another fracture, another scar added to the tally.
My faith went through all stages: deepening, cracking under pressure, reshaping into something new. It never truly disappeared, but sometimes it croaked like a bridge under tremendous weight. I came out of this more evolved, more mature, with a sharper sense of what life means. Every day, I told myself: Never give up. Never take no for an answer. Persevere.
I had dark thoughts. There were moments I wanted to die, when it seemed easier than facing endless pain and loss. But I kept living for those who loved me, who wanted me to survive. I poured myself into writing letters, processing the chaos through words on a page, letting language keep me tethered to hope.
And yet, even in the depths of this nightmare, absurdity found me. Like the time I got stuck on the toilet and the entire fire department had to come rescue me. It’s a ridiculous memory, but it’s proof that laughter survives even in the worst of times.
Rehab sessions were painful and grueling every single day. There’s no soft way to say it. Every motion felt like tearing open wounds that were barely healing. But I kept going. The first goal that burned in my mind was driving. I wanted my independence back. I wanted to feel the road under my wheels again, to remind myself that I was still moving forward—even if it wasn’t on a motorcycle.
This chapter of my life wasn’t glamorous. It was raw and bruised and bloody. But it was the beginning of rising from the wreckage, piece by piece.
