====== Fuck This Week - 08-11-2025 ======

This past week didn’t just knock the wind out of me — it shoved years of betrayal, manipulation, theft, and loss into the same room, shut the door, and made me sit with them. And sitting there, staring them all in the face, I saw every red flag I’d missed, every warning sign I’d told myself wasn’t what it looked like. The first one had been right there at the start. I wasn’t a wife. I was a roommate. For four years I lived with his family, sleeping in a bed that never felt like mine, eating at a table that never felt like ours. “It’s just for now,” I told myself. “It’s cultural, it’s temporary.” It wasn’t. It was permanent, and it was the shape of everything to come.
I married him not knowing the truth about who he really was. Years later, I’d be standing in the hallway with a stack of worn papers in my hands, my thumb running over the bent corners. Fake identity documents. The ink smelled faintly of plastic sleeves and dust. He’d used these to get into the country, to become the man I thought I knew. Before I could wrap my head around that, my mind jumped to the other thing I’d always known deep down — that our marriage had been hollow. We had sex only three times. Three. Even then, he was distant, his eyes sliding away from mine, his body halfway somewhere else. I used to think it was stress or maybe something medical, that maybe I wasn’t enough. Now I know I was never the problem. His intimacy was reserved for others.
His phone was the evidence I didn’t want to find. A screen glowing in the dark, hundreds of contacts that looked harmless until you saw the pattern: “Bro,” “Atlanta,” “Chicago.” My stomach tightened as I scrolled. Not friends. Not business. Lovers. So many that my eyes blurred from the scrolling. And still, I stayed, telling myself stories to make the lies fit.
The day before my accident in 2022, all those excuses shattered. I opened a door and froze. The first thing I noticed was the sound — wet, rhythmic, unmistakable. Then my eyes caught up, and there he was, pants low, head tilted back, another man on his knees. My mouth went dry. For a second I just stood there, the air thick and heavy around me. “Are you serious?” I finally said, my voice thin and sharp. He didn’t move. Didn’t stop. Just stared at me with this strange calm, like I was the one out of place. I walked away, my heart pounding so hard it hurt. Less than twenty-four hours later, none of it mattered.
The crash came in metal and asphalt and sirens. The world blurred to a hospital ceiling, the beep of machines, the burn of IV lines in my arm. I was crippled, told I’d have to learn to walk again — twice. Every night was antiseptic and shadows, nurses’ shoes squeaking on tile, my legs heavy and useless under the sheets. And while I was lying there, holding on to life, he was with someone else. I didn’t find out right away. But when I did, the betrayal felt like being hit all over again, this time in the chest.
The truth unraveled in pieces. This wasn’t about love. It never had been. His plan from the beginning had been to have children through surrogacy, leave me, and raise them alone. I’d sunk over $250,000 into that process, chasing something that was never mine to have. When it failed, I thought it was the deepest kind of loss. Now I see it as luck in disguise. No child caught in this wreckage. No rope tying me to him forever.
I’d fought for every penny I had. My accident settlement — a million dollars — bought the house outright. I thought that meant security. But he never contributed. Every cent of his income went to India, while I carried everything else: the house, the bills, the surrogacy costs, my business, the trades, all of it. Then Trump’s tariffs hit. My business faltered. My investments bled dry. And the slow leak became a flood. Now the house I bought with my own blood money is tangled in a legal dispute I could lose.
As the marriage rotted, the attacks got bolder. My home was ransacked three times — not by strangers, but by him and my ex-friend, Sherubah. The first time, I came home to drawers gutted, clothes scattered like dead leaves, a faint smell of cigarette smoke woven into the curtains. My gold was gone. So were my keepsakes, my pens — including Dr. Whitaker’s pen, a small thing with big meaning from the man who’d set me on my healthcare IT career in 2012. The second time, the mess was worse, the destruction more deliberate. And once, they even tried to drown my cat. That thought still makes my hands shake. It wasn’t just theft. It was psychological warfare, and it worked. I spent nights sleeping with the lights on, listening for sounds that meant they were back.
Then came the police. Not as protection, but as another weapon. He knew how to twist the story so I was the one under the microscope. Out in public, his story was simple: I was a drug addict surrounded by other addicts. The truth was messier. I’d had four years of solid sobriety before my accident. After 2022, I slipped, but not into the pit he describes. I call it “California sober.” Not perfect, but not lost.
One of the few people I’d let in during all of this was a friend I met on a street corner when I had no one else. He showed up in my life deep in fentanyl addiction. He’s tried to help me, and I’ve tried to help him, but his addiction drags its own chaos through my door. I know how to get clean. I wish he wanted it too.
Somewhere in this wreckage, I met my current boyfriend in the psych ward. We are two damaged people who don’t hide it from each other. Yes, we are codependent. Yes, we lean too hard sometimes. But we are trying to learn another way. It’s strange, being in love while devastated, like holding your lover in a cardboard box while the rain seeps through the seams and you’re both wondering if tomorrow brings shelter or another storm.
Now I stand here disabled, broke, staring down the loss of my home, divorcing a man who betrayed me before, during, and after the worst moment of my life, a man who used me financially, emotionally, and legally. I didn’t see the signs when I should have. But I see them now. And even with almost nothing left, I’m still here. Still breathing. Still telling the truth. And somehow, even in the ruins, still capable of love.
