====== Noises ======

It wasn’t just fear of people or conspiracies. The worst triggers were often the simplest things: repetitive noises. The thump of a hammer, the hum of machinery, even the ticking of a clock could feel like nails dragging down a chalkboard inside my skull. Those sounds burrowed into my brain until I wanted to claw my own skin to make them stop. It’s part of why my PTSD took the shape it did—because after my accident and the chaos of mold remediation and machines roaring through my home, my nervous system learned to equate noise with danger.
I started dissociating more often, especially during drives. I’d be behind the wheel one moment, then blink and realize I’d lost miles of road with no memory of how I got there. Sometimes I’d pull over, trembling, gripping the steering wheel as though it could anchor me back to reality.
Meanwhile, the physical pain remained relentless. My leg throbbed like it held a second heartbeat. My back ached with every breath. Scar tissue pulled tight across my skin, a constant reminder of how violently my body had been broken.
Therapy became my lifeline. Some days I sat silent, unable to put my chaos into words. Other days, I spilled everything in frantic sentences, desperate for someone to help me sort reality from terror. My therapist was the one person who kept gently reminding me that the world wasn’t always out to get me—that I wasn’t alone, no matter how loud my mind screamed otherwise.
Even then, tiny sparks of hope flickered through the darkness. My husband’s text messages telling me he loved me. The warmth of coffee in my hands. The rare, unexpected burst of laughter. Those moments were proof that my story wasn’t finished yet.
And every day, I repeated the same stubborn mantra: Never give up.
