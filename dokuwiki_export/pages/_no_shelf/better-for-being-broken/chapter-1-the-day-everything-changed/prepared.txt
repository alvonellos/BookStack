====== Prepared ======

In hindsight, I feel that God prepared me for that day.
My motorcycle wasn’t even at my house that morning—it was at the shop. I’d Ubered over to pick it up, thinking it was just a routine errand. While I was there, I made sure everything was perfect for the season ahead: new brakes, new tires, new fairings, a fresh chain. I even bought myself a brand-new helmet—top of the line—and a new jacket and gloves. It was like God knew to prepare me with the best equipment. At the time, I thought I was just being careful. Now I think it was divine foresight.
I rode the bike home, exhilarated to have it back. My husband begged me not to go out again that day. He asked me to stay home, to just leave it parked. But I felt like I had to get back on it. I wanted to go to the post office and to an AA meeting down the street at 8111 Roswell Road, not far from my house.
So I went for the ride that almost cost me my life, and everything changed.
I was less than half a mile from home when it happened. You already know the basics: a side street, a sudden intrusion into my lane. But there’s more to it than that.
It was a rideshare driver, in the middle of a fare. It turned out to be an enormous settlement afterward, though I’m not allowed to discuss the details. The vehicle was a Jeep truck.
The responding officer was a trans man who did a thorough investigation. That’s how they ultimately found the driver at fault—because he lied about what happened. Someday I’ll upload the transcripts of the audio from the accident. The truth is all there, recorded in sirens and strained voices.
I remember it all so vividly: I was changing lanes. Then he suddenly jerked into my lane from a dead stop, trying to jump ahead of a few cars. And in that split second, I realized I had three options. Do nothing and crash over the top. Try to duck under his bumper and risk being run over. Or swerve around him.
I tried to go around him, but there was too little distance. So I did what I thought might save me: I locked my rear brake and laid my bike down, trying to let go. But my bike caught me instead. It grabbed me like a metal claw and slammed me into the back of the truck.
The impact was catastrophic. My leg was fractured completely. My pelvis was broken. Five vertebrae were fractured. My collarbone snapped in two places. It all happened in a blink, yet it felt like forever.
I was wearing all my gear. In that moment, I felt prepared. Even as I lay there, pain screaming through my body, I remember thinking that if I hadn’t bought that helmet, that jacket, those gloves—that gear—I’d be dead.
When I woke up, I tried to move, but people were swarming around me, telling me not to. My right leg was dangling, held together only by sinews and skin. It looked like it wasn’t even part of me anymore.
I don’t remember the ambulance ride to the hospital. Everything after the crash is a blur. I do remember being in the ER, the lights blinding me, voices overlapping in a chaotic orchestra of medical urgency. I remember being K-holed—given so much ketamine it could have dropped a horse—while they set my leg.
Somewhere in that haze, as pain and drugs battled for control of my mind, I realized that my life would never be the same again.
When the drugs wore off, reality came crashing back like a cold wave. I opened my eyes to a room I didn’t recognize, full of machines blinking and beeping. The sterile smell of antiseptic hung in the air like a warning. My mouth was dry, my head heavy. And my legs—my legs felt like they were floating somewhere far away, not connected to me at all.
A nurse leaned over me and said, “Don’t try to move yet.” Her voice was gentle, but her eyes were full of something close to pity. I hated that look. I wanted to scream that I wasn’t fragile. That I was still me. But I couldn’t find the words.
The surgeon came in later, speaking in a careful, measured voice. He told me I would walk again—but it would be challenging. There was nothing they could do surgically for my pelvis, spine, or collarbone; those injuries would have to heal on their own, which meant weeks of lying still and barely moving. But they had placed an intramedullary rod in my right leg to stabilize what he called a class IIIc tibial fracture—so severe that if it had been any worse, they would have amputated my leg.
He said I would need multiple skin grafts—not from road rash, but because the bone in my leg had exploded outward, tearing through flesh and skin. His words hung heavy in the air, turning the sterile room into a place that felt both alien and terrifying.
I thought about my husband. About my family. About my job. About the life I’d worked so hard to build. And underneath it all was a quiet terror that whispered: Who am I if I can’t walk?
The next days blurred together into a fog of painkillers and half-sleep. Friends and family drifted in and out, faces lined with worry. Nurses adjusted tubes and machines. My husband held my hand and tried to smile, even when his eyes were rimmed with red.
Late at night, when the hospital was quiet, I lay awake and listened to the hum of the fluorescent lights. I tried to wiggle my toes. Some nights, I thought I felt a flicker of movement. Other nights, nothing at all.
That was when I first realized that resilience wasn’t about feeling brave. It was about refusing to surrender to the idea that this was the end. Even when part of me wanted to close my eyes and never wake up again, another part of me kept whispering: This is not how it ends.
I didn’t know then how long the road ahead would be. Or that I’d have to learn to walk twice. All I knew was that I wasn’t ready to give up. Not yet.
The days turned into weeks. I became an unwilling expert in pain management, surgical drains, wound vacs, and the sharp sting of dressings being changed. The wound vacs were loud and relentless, tubes snaking from my leg to a machine that sucked away fluids, reminding me with every hum and gurgle how close I’d come to losing my leg. The sound was constant, drilling into my brain until I thought I’d scream.
While I was fighting to heal, there was chaos waiting for me at home. Countless people were in and out of my house—nurses, physical therapists, contractors. At the same time, my home was under massive mold and asbestos remediation. Machines roared and fans blasted day and night to clear the air. The banging of hammers, the whine of saws, the thump of boots on floors—it all blurred together, feeding into the PTSD that still makes repetitive noises feel like an ambush.
Time lost all meaning. The sun rose and set beyond the narrow hospital window, but my world was reduced to ceiling tiles and the steady beep of monitors. Sometimes I wondered if this was what the rest of my life would be: trapped in a body that refused to obey me, defined by injuries and limitations. Other times, I clung to small victories, like the first time I managed to shift my weight in bed without crying out. The nurses cheered me on like I’d won an Olympic medal.
Physical therapists started coming into my room. They were cheerful, persistent, and entirely unsympathetic to my protests. They explained I’d have to relearn everything—from sitting up to standing, from taking a single step to climbing stairs. The pain was indescribable, sharp and electric, radiating through my leg and back with every tiny motion. But with each excruciating session, I kept repeating to myself: This is not how it ends.
Meanwhile, the reality of my injuries set in. Skin grafts were scheduled. Doctors measured patches of my thighs to harvest skin for my leg. I never thought I’d be discussing which parts of my own body could be sacrificed to save the rest of me. The thought made my stomach churn.
Despite the physical agony, the hardest part was the mental war. I couldn’t help imagining all the things I might never do again: hiking trails, dancing with my husband, riding my motorcycle. Some days, grief hit me in tidal waves, leaving me gasping for air. Other days, I felt strangely peaceful—as if I was floating outside myself, observing this new version of my life from afar.
One night, unable to sleep, I whispered a prayer into the darkness. I asked God for strength—not to be instantly healed, but simply to endure whatever came next. And somehow, deep in my chest, I felt the faintest spark of certainty that I wasn’t alone.
This wasn’t how my story would end. Not if I had anything to say about it.
Eventually, the doctors decided I was stable enough to leave the hospital. But “going home” wasn’t the victory I’d imagined. I was wheeled out through the same doors I’d come in, but I was not the same person. My body was stitched, stapled, and pieced together with metal rods. My spirit felt fragile, like thin glass vibrating under a high note.
My home, which I’d once thought of as my sanctuary, had become another battlefield. The mold and asbestos crews were still working, their machines roaring like engines. Plastic sheeting hung in doorways, creating tunnels through my own living room. The smell of chemicals and dust filled the air. Nurses and therapists came and went, checking wounds, adjusting bandages, tending to my wound vac. I felt like a patient in a makeshift hospital rather than a person living in their own home.
The wound vac remained a constant companion. Its low mechanical drone was a soundtrack to my days and haunted my nights. Sometimes, when I closed my eyes, I heard its suction pulse echoing in my ears. Even now, certain hums or mechanical beeps can send me straight back to those days, heart racing, breath shallow.
At times, I thought I was losing my mind. Noise triggers blended with physical pain until I felt cornered in my own skin. My husband tried his best to soothe me, but there were nights I’d wake screaming, convinced I was still trapped beneath that Jeep truck or lying in a hospital bed.
And yet, even amid the chaos, there were moments of stubborn hope. A physical therapist helped me stand beside my hospital bed for the first time, my weight supported by a walker and sheer willpower. The world tilted, my vision blurred, and I nearly blacked out. But I was upright. I was vertical. And everyone in the room clapped like I’d summited Everest.
It was a reminder: my story wasn’t over. My body was broken, but my spirit was still fighting. And I intended to keep fighting—for my freedom, for my marriage, for the life I refused to surrender.
Every day at home felt like a test. There were wound checks, bandage changes, and endless discussions of how my skin grafts were healing. My right leg was a map of scars, stitches, and fresh pink skin that looked nothing like the leg I used to know. I tried not to look at it too long. Sometimes it felt like staring at someone else’s body.
Physical therapy became my new job. My therapist was relentless, but in the way you secretly hope for. She refused to let me give up. We started with small things: bending my knee a few degrees, lifting my foot an inch off the ground. Each movement felt like someone setting fire to my nerves. My back screamed every time I shifted weight. My pelvis sent jolts of pain shooting up my spine. But I kept going because the alternative was unthinkable.
In the quiet moments between appointments and construction noise, I found myself thinking about the person I was before the accident. I missed riding my motorcycle with the wind against my face. I missed moving through the world without calculating every step, every transfer, every possibility of falling. I missed the feeling of my body obeying me without question.
There were days when despair threatened to swallow me whole. I’d stare at the wound vac tubing and wonder how much more I could take. But then my husband would remind me how far I’d come, how I was beating odds the doctors hadn’t dared to predict. He’d help me laugh about the absurdity of our house looking like a hazmat zone, and sometimes that laughter was the only thing that kept me going.
Each day, I reminded myself that this wasn’t how my story would end. And though I couldn’t see it yet, I was beginning to believe that maybe—just maybe—I could become better for being broken.
Recovery wasn’t a straight line. There were setbacks, infections, days where the pain spiked so high I could barely breathe. There were moments when I believed I’d never be normal again, that the best parts of my life were behind me. But even on those days, there was a flicker of defiance inside me—a voice that refused to let me surrender.
Gradually, tiny victories stacked up. I took a few steps with a walker. I managed to sit upright for longer than ten minutes. I learned how to shower on my own again. Each milestone felt impossibly small and impossibly huge at the same time.
The wound vac eventually came off. The house repairs finished. The noise subsided. The dust settled, literally and figuratively. My body was scarred and changed, but it was still mine. And as the days stretched into months, I realized that I, too, was still here.
I’m not the same person who swung a leg over that motorcycle on a sunny day and sped down Roswell Road. I’m different now. Softer in some places, harder in others. I’ve learned that resilience isn’t about pretending you’re unbreakable. It’s about letting yourself break—and then choosing, over and over again, to heal.
So this is how my story begins. Not with perfection, but with shattered bones, roaring machines, and an unshakable determination to keep moving forward. And though my scars will always remind me of that day, they also remind me of this truth:
I’m better for being broken.
