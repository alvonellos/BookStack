====== A brief talk on disability ======

1. Introduction: The Early Struggles
From an early age, I knew my mind and body worked differently. As someone who lives with both mental and physical disabilities, navigating the world wasn't always straightforward, and about two years ago it came to a head when I had a catastrophic motorcycle accident, the consequences which completely changed how I saw the world. The truth was, I was running from something on that bike, and ever since I came back, I came back ready to face it and I have been guided like a missile. 
Growing up, I was always aware that I was different from everyone else. I was home-schooled, I grew up in a military family in the peak 90's early 2000's, just as the Internet age began to boom. At that time, I had no physical pain or understanding of what pain was, but I still dealt with mental health struggles and the general malaise caused by really just being so critically different and lost. 
 It wasn’t until I was older that I started to understand the invisible toll mental health can take, especially on one’s ability to function in high-pressure environments like IT. You can know the computer science all day long, but can you hack it while you're under the gun? For me, this became a challenge all of a sudden. I no longer process epinephrine correctly. Something changed in my body as a result of what happened. I can't fix it. 
In the beginning, I tried to deny it. Who wants to admit that they’re struggling? But ignoring it only made things worse. Eventually, I had to face my reality, accepting that my mind and body weren’t going to cooperate with me in the ways I thought they would, and had in fact betrayed me.
   But this wasn’t a defeat, it was an awakening. It was an opportunity to rethink how I lived and worked. And it would become the foundation for a much more unconventional career in technology by thinking outside the box.
2. The Intersection of Disabilities and IT
The world of IT is fast-paced, demanding, and relentless. It’s a place where innovation and precision are prized, and where long hours in front of screens often leave little room for personal challenges. I quickly learned that my disabilities weren’t something I could hide in this field; they became a part of my story and influenced how I approached my work. Ultimately, ignoring them was the biggest mistake I had made in my career. When it was just ADHD, I failed at communication, I failed at organization, I failed at handling the low moments but absolutely killed it when there was a fire under my ass, I failed in many ways. 
  Most of my IT career has been spent with solely the mental part as a factor, and I did a very good job at masking most of the time, and my non-linear abstract thinking about problems has been a boon. Then I had my accident. I was lucky to be alive. There were a lot of things I was told I would not be able to do, but I now know what it means to do in spite of someone. Usually, it's because of someone or something, but sometimes, it's about taking what you have in an imperfect world and assembling with it the best you can cobble together. 
   I have never held a corporate IT job, but I have been hired on by corporate IT to fix what's broken in their organization. I did this before I broached into the corporate space in 2020, finally, as a software engineer, and spent the next four years trying to change the world through a consulting career, honing, and evolving on everything that I have been doing beforehand. Software development seemed like a natural progression, but I kept hitting road blocks. 
 These road blocks are stopping me from being complacent for too long. I broached into the corporate space in 2020 when I joined Accenture and spent the next four years building a consulting career, honing and evolving on everything that I have been doing beforehand. The software development was a natural progression, and this is where I am at now, but I am on a sabbatical currently while I figure some things out. I am super passionate about what I do, but I don't feel like there's an alignment on goals and career path. 
   There were days when the physical exhaustion felt overwhelming. Chronic pain made it hard to sit at my desk for long hours, and my mental health struggles sometimes left me feeling mentally foggy or disconnected. I sit for fifteen minutes and my leg starts jolting me from where I had a nerve graft and major surgery. I can't concentrate on anything I am doing. I stand for a minute, and my spine throbs. I lay down, I twitch until I fall asleep. No idea.
  It's a weird feeling to be happy that you're alive, and understand what it means to have everything near and dear taken away, but also it's hard to stay happy when everything I do causes extreme pain and suffering. It does not matter whether you are active or still.
But what I didn’t realize at the time was that these struggles forced me to rethink how I approached tasks and how I value them; unfortunately, I had to learn this the hard way and through exhaustive search. 
  At first, I approached it with sheer determination. It is the equivalent of putting your hand on the burner and trying to convince yourself it doesn't hurt. I was successful in this for about a year just out of sheer stubbornness. This is what got me through learning to walk twice, getting back into my job, and figuring out how to navigate this world. If viewed in terms of a physical sense of tolerance, I was using my brainpower to compensate, and it fractured my mind.
  September of 2023 I woke up one day, and my husband and I got into it. I just realized I couldn't do it anymore. 
  I spent four months in psychiatric trying to understand why I am having convulsions and panic attacks and sweats and why I am so angry all the time when I have never been this way before and how to deal with it and save my life. 
  This was the first time in my life I entered the mental health system, and the first time I got some answers and help. Many, many, many acronyms. I received several diagnoses. I learned so much but most of all I learned this idea of radical acceptance and it enabled me to let go and let God the things that I was running from on that bike in the first place. 

3. Building Resilience
Resilience isn’t something that comes naturally to everyone—it’s something you build, piece by piece, over time. For me, resilience became a necessity. Each day, I was faced with the challenge of overcoming both the physical and mental barriers that could have easily slowed me down or caused me to give up. But resilience became the cornerstone of my career and my personal life.
I learned early on that I couldn’t do it all at once. I had to break things down into smaller, more manageable tasks. Instead of attempting to solve an entire complex issue in one go, I’d focus on smaller milestones. Every task I completed, no matter how small, was a victory—a testament to my ability to push through and keep going.
The mental strain of chronic conditions often left me feeling drained, but I found ways to recharge. Whether it was taking time for self-care, exercising to relieve stress, or practicing mindfulness, I developed a toolkit of strategies to help me cope. It wasn’t always easy, and sometimes the struggle felt relentless. But each time I encountered a setback, I grew stronger. I learned that resilience isn’t about never falling; it’s about getting back up each time you do.
This mindset was crucial when it came to facing the constant challenges of the IT field. There are always setbacks—projects that don’t go as planned, systems that fail unexpectedly, and clients who don’t understand the intricacies of your work. But when you have learned to overcome your own struggles, those professional hurdles feel a little more manageable. Resilience, for me, wasn’t just a way of coping—it became a way of thriving.

4. Shaping Your Problem-Solving Skills
Having mental and physical disabilities forced me to approach problem-solving differently from the start. While many people in IT are trained to solve problems with linear, conventional methods, I learned early that flexibility and creativity were my greatest assets. I couldn’t always rely on brute force to push through; I had to find smarter, more efficient ways to get things done.
For example, during long coding sessions, when the physical pain or mental fatigue would hit, I couldn’t keep my focus on a single screen for hours. But this limitation led me to discover new ways of working, such as breaking down projects into smaller pieces and using multiple tools and workspaces to stay engaged. I learned to think in systems—how can I break this problem down so that I can work on it in manageable chunks, without overloading myself? This ability to think in layers, to compartmentalize and focus on one thing at a time, became one of my greatest strengths in the IT world.
I also learned to trust my intuition. In moments when I was physically or mentally drained, I would often find that my subconscious mind had already solved the problem, if only I gave myself the space to let it unfold. The distractions of pain or fatigue became a reminder to listen more closely to my instincts and the deeper patterns I had built over the years. It wasn’t always a direct approach, but it was often an efficient one.
This thinking outside of traditional solutions didn’t just apply to coding—it permeated every aspect of my work. Whether it was finding creative ways to solve technical issues or negotiating with clients, I relied on the adaptability I developed from living with my disabilities. The limitations of my body and mind had pushed me to innovate, and in many ways, that made me a better problem-solver in IT.

5. The Impact on Relationships and Mentorship
Navigating my disabilities while working in IT also deeply affected my relationships—both with colleagues and with the people I mentored. At times, it was difficult to open up about my struggles, but I found that being transparent helped me build stronger, more authentic connections. It wasn’t about making excuses for why I couldn’t perform at my best every day; it was about helping others understand the context in which I was working, and the additional layers of effort required to perform at my peak.
With my colleagues, I became known as someone who understood the importance of balance. I couldn’t push through every obstacle with brute force, and that awareness led me to encourage my teammates to take breaks, prioritize self-care, and work more strategically. We started to develop a culture of support, where vulnerability was seen as a strength, not a weakness. This approach helped us to be more empathetic and collaborative.
As a mentor, my own experiences shaped how I supported others. I was able to share not just technical knowledge but also insights on how to manage stress, maintain mental health, and deal with the inevitable failures that come with working in such a dynamic field. I spoke openly about my journey and how resilience wasn’t just about physical strength but about mental adaptability. For those facing their own challenges, I became a resource for both professional growth and personal development.
Over time, I realized that my story was an important one to share—not just for my own growth, but for the growth of those around me. My relationships with colleagues and mentees became more grounded in mutual respect, understanding, and a shared commitment to pushing forward despite the obstacles in our way.

6. Turning Struggles into Strengths
Looking back, I see that the very challenges I once saw as obstacles were, in many ways, the catalysts for my growth. My physical and mental struggles didn’t just make me stronger—they gave me a unique perspective on both technology and life. In a field that demands constant problem-solving, my disabilities taught me that it’s not always about overcoming every obstacle with force; sometimes, it’s about finding new ways to navigate the world around you.
I began to understand that my struggles had a purpose—they helped me develop qualities that made me not only a better IT professional but a more empathetic and effective leader. The patience required to deal with chronic pain taught me to be more thoughtful and deliberate in my decision-making. The mental fog, while frustrating, made me more mindful of the need for clarity and focus. I learned to celebrate small victories because I understood that every step forward was significant, no matter how seemingly minor.
My ability to push through despite physical and mental fatigue gave me a deep sense of resilience, but more than that, it gave me a sense of purpose. I realized that my experiences were valuable, not just in terms of personal growth, but also in terms of how I could help others. I started to embrace the idea that strength isn’t about avoiding weakness—it’s about integrating it, learning from it, and using it to fuel your success.
As I grew in my career, I took pride in the way my struggles shaped my work ethic and my relationships. I wasn’t just surviving in the IT field—I was thriving, using every lesson from my disabilities to become the professional I am today. My challenges became the very foundation of my strength, and in the end, they empowered me to achieve far more than I had ever imagined.

7. A Call to Others Facing Similar Challenges
If there’s one thing I’ve learned through my journey with mental and physical disabilities, it’s that challenges don’t define us—our response to them does. To anyone facing their own obstacles, whether in the IT field or beyond, I want to share this message: Your struggles are not a limitation; they are part of your unique journey, and they can fuel your success if you let them.
There will be days when it feels like the weight of your challenges is too much to bear, when the world of IT seems unforgiving and relentless. But in those moments, remember that the strength you need is already within you. You don’t need to be perfect; you just need to keep moving forward. Celebrate your progress, no matter how small, and recognize that the journey is just as important as the destination.
To those who feel like they don’t belong, who struggle with mental health or chronic pain, know that you are not alone. I’ve been there, and I can tell you that it is possible to build a successful career while managing these challenges. Find your support system, whether that’s in the form of a mentor, a colleague, or a community of like-minded individuals who understand what you’re going through. Surround yourself with people who lift you up, who see the potential in you even when you don’t see it in yourself.
And finally, remember that resilience doesn’t mean never falling down—it means getting back up, every single time. Whether you're battling fatigue, mental fog, or self-doubt, your journey is a testament to your strength. It is possible to thrive in the face of adversity, and you can redefine what success looks like on your own terms.
Keep pushing forward. Your challenges are the very things that will make you stronger, wiser, and more capable than you ever imagined.
